*This study is made up of twelve short devotionals. Each one begins with the Bible passages to read first. Scripture quotations are from the World English Bible (WEB).*

## 1. The Lord Gave, and the Lord Has Taken Away

**Read:** Job 1 · James 5:11

We don't know how old the book of Job is, and we don't know who wrote it. The story takes place in the land of Uz, which some believe was Edom. The book is counted among the wisdom literature of the Bible. Still, I'm convinced that Job really existed. He is even mentioned in the letter of James:

> “Behold, we call them blessed who endured. You have heard of the perseverance of Job and have seen the Lord in the outcome, and how the Lord is full of compassion and mercy.” (James 5:11)

God calls Job “blameless and upright,” yet we know Job wasn't without sin. He himself acknowledges the sins of his youth (Job 13:26). Even so, Job is blessed in every way. He has ten children: seven sons and three daughters. He is wealthy, owns enormous herds of livestock, and has a spotless reputation.

The story opens in heaven. The “sons of God” come to present themselves before the Lord. This phrase appears elsewhere in the Old Testament too, for example in Job 38:7, and it refers to heavenly beings. Satan is among them. The Hebrew text says *ha-satan*, which means “the accuser.” So Satan is in no way God's equal, not even as His opponent. He has to ask God's permission to do anything at all. In the end he gets that permission, on one condition: he may not lay a hand on Job himself. Satan is convinced that Job only honors God because he has been blessed so richly.

And so he strikes Job, in a terrible way. One messenger after another arrives with dreadful news. Job's livestock is stolen and some of his servants are killed. Then fire falls from the sky and kills his other servants and his sheep. As if that weren't enough, all ten of his children die when the house they are in collapses on top of them. It is too much to take in. Job tears his robe and shaves his head, a common expression of deep grief and despair in the ancient Near East.

Job immediately understands that this is no coincidence. Something else is going on here. He attributes everything he has ever received to God, and everything that has now been taken from him as well. And still he praises God's name:

> “Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked will I return there. Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be Yahweh's name.” In all this, Job didn't sin, nor charge God with wrongdoing. (Job 1:21–22)

That is incredible, under these circumstances.

**Reflect:** It may be hard for us to imagine. But if everything you hold dear were suddenly taken away, would you still be able to honor God, like Job did?

## 2. When Nothing Makes Sense Anymore

**Read:** Job 2 · Job 3:1–8 · Isaiah 27:1 · Psalm 74:14 · Psalm 104:26 · Deuteronomy 28:35

Now Job's friends are introduced. Eliphaz comes from Teman, southeast of Israel. Bildad is from Shuah, probably a tribe in northwestern Arabia. Zophar likely comes from northern Arabia or Jordan. They were probably educated men, and they are certainly eloquent. When they arrive, they don't even recognize Job: his face and body are covered in sores.

They sit with him in silence for seven days, following an ancient Near Eastern ritual that is still known today in Judaism as *shiva*. In Genesis 50:10 we also read about seven days of mourning for Jacob. It is a beautiful tradition, one that gives silence, and the bearing of deep grief over Job's children, all the room it needs. In this ritual, the mourner is the first to speak. And eventually Job does:

> “Let the day perish in which I was born, the night which said, ‘There is a boy conceived.’” (Job 3:3)

Nothing makes sense anymore. Job has always lived rightly. He has been good to orphans and to the poor. He was known as a wise and just man. And now, suddenly, everything that made him feel blessed is gone. His children are dead, and with them his legacy. His wealth is gone. His wife even tells him to curse God and die. Those are very harsh words, and perhaps hard for us to understand. But she, too, has lost ten children, and now she watches her husband suffer terribly from his sores. She seems to have lost all hope.

Finally, Job loses his health as well. He suffers terribly. Physically, because of the sores, which were associated with impurity and even with a curse; Deuteronomy 28:35 speaks of exactly that. But Job suffers most of all in his mind. He doesn't understand. How can a righteous God do this? Has he overlooked something?

In his deepest despair, Job says:

> “Let them curse it who curse the day, who are ready to rouse up leviathan.” (Job 3:8)

That sounds strange to us, but in the ancient Near East everyone knew exactly what Leviathan was: a horrifying sea monster, sometimes called a dragon, that symbolized pure chaos. Leviathan also appears in Isaiah 27 and in the Psalms:

> “There the ships go, and leviathan, whom you formed to play there.” (Psalm 104:26)

> “You broke the heads of leviathan in pieces. You gave him as food to people and desert creatures.” (Psalm 74:14)

So what Job is really saying is this: nothing makes sense anymore, so let the whole world sink into chaos. Let it be handed over to the elements, to complete randomness. Job doesn't understand, and he curses the day he was born. Where has his God of order gone?

## 3. Looking for Someone to Blame

**Read:** Job 4–5 · Matthew 10:29

Now Job's friends respond to him. Eliphaz is the first, in chapter 4. Among other things, he says:

> “According to what I have seen, those who plow iniquity, and sow trouble, reap the same.” (Job 4:8)

In other words: Job has brought this on himself through his own actions. What a brutal thing for this grieving father to hear, a man who has lost ten children and everything he owned. Eliphaz's words come from a typical ancient Near Eastern way of thinking, a transactional view of life: if this much disaster strikes you, you must have done something wrong.

Then, in verse 12, Eliphaz says something striking: “Now a thing was secretly brought to me.” The Hebrew word used here comes from *ganab*, which carries the sense of something stolen, sneaky, done in secret. Eliphaz describes a spirit that passed before his face, making every hair on his body stand on end. It's terrifying, and it almost sounds like an episode of sleep paralysis. Some commentators believe this message did not come from God, partly because of the frightening and unclear nature of the vision. The conclusion of this spirit's words is that suffering is the result of sin:

> “Shall mortal man be more just than God? Shall a man be more pure than his Maker? Behold, he puts no trust in his servants. He charges his angels with error. How much more, those who dwell in houses of clay, whose foundation is in the dust!” (Job 4:17–19)

And all the while, Job is suffering because of a trial that Satan is carrying out with God's permission.

Later, in verse 20, Eliphaz says this about human fate: “They perish forever without any regarding it.” As if the world were ruled by total indifference. Thankfully, we know that isn't true. Nothing escapes God. Jesus says about the sparrows:

> “Not one of them falls on the ground apart from your Father's will.” (Matthew 10:29)

This seems to be a spiritual battle. Eliphaz attacks Job's intentions and motives, and he uses his own nightmare as an argument, while Job is already broken and has nothing left to lose. These harsh words make Job's situation so much heavier. On top of the physical and emotional pain comes something new: being accused, as if he caused all of this himself.

In chapter 5, verse 7, Eliphaz says that “man is born to trouble, as the sparks fly upward.” The Hebrew literally says “as the sons of Resheph fly upward.” Resheph was the Canaanite god of plague. An interesting expression, but hardly an encouraging one for Job.

**Reflect:** Do you recognize in yourself the urge to look for a cause when someone else is suffering?

## 4. A Theology of Grace

**Read:** Job 6

Job responds to Eliphaz, and his pain is palpable. All he wants is comfort and kindness from his friends. Instead, he gets rebuke.

How easy it is sometimes, when we are confronted with serious suffering around us, to start looking for a cause right away. Something outside of ourselves: a reason, something that might explain why this is happening to someone. Because it is hard to live with the thought that it simply happens, for no reason at all. If that is the case, it could happen to us too. But when we respond like that, God's blessing is not on it.

Job makes a sharp observation in chapter 6, verse 21: “You see a terror, and are afraid.” And he's right. To his friends, he has become a frightening example of a person on whom every possible disaster has fallen at once.

But Job shows something different. He has a theology of grace. This is how he sees his suffering: God gave, and God has taken away. The blessings in his life were never a “wage” he earned by being so obedient. He sees them as a gift. A gift that God gives, and that God can take away again if He chooses.

That is what sets him apart from his friends. They believe the ancient Near Eastern dogma: people must obey so that the gods will protect them from evil, and as soon as you sin, all kinds of disaster can come your way. You can hear it in their speeches. They accuse Job of being wicked and guilty, and they even try to get him to confess something, so that he might still avert some of this disaster.

Job begins to break free from that way of thinking, though not without stumbling. Yes, he wrestles with God and with “the terrors of God” that “set themselves in array against me” (6:4). He speaks about it openly:

> “Oh that my anguish were weighed, and all my calamity laid in the balances! For now it would be heavier than the sand of the seas, therefore have my words been rash.” (Job 6:2–3)

At the same time, he loves God for who He is, not for what He gives. Not once in the text does Job ask for his possessions back. He doesn't weep over his lost wealth. He grieves because he doesn't understand why God is doing this, because he has lost his relationship with his God.

## 5. I Know That My Redeemer Lives

**Read:** Job 16:13 · Job 19:1–8 · Job 19:23–27 · Psalm 16:7 · Psalm 139:13

> “Oh that my words were now written! Oh that they were inscribed in a book! That with an iron pen and lead they were engraved in the rock forever!” (Job 19:23–24)

Job is struggling with the beliefs and reactions of his friends. They seem convinced that he has done something wrong, and they accuse him without mercy. We see Job's emotions swing in every direction.

On the one hand, he sees God as the one who is causing him all this pain, and he experiences no help and no justice. In chapter 16, verse 13, Job says: “He splits my kidneys apart, and does not spare.” In the ancient Near East, the kidneys had a special meaning. David writes in the Psalms that his kidneys instruct him in the night (Psalm 16:7; many translations say “heart”). The kidneys stood for the core of the personality, a person's innermost being. The Talmud says: the kidneys give counsel, the heart understands, the tongue speaks. We tend to connect our identity with our brain, but God looks at the heart. He formed our kidneys, our inmost being (Psalm 139:13). So Job feels struck in the very depths of who he is.

On the other hand, there is a deep conviction in him that God is his Redeemer. And that word carries special meaning. In Hebrew it is *go'el*, the kinsman-redeemer. In the ancient Near East, the *go'el* had the legal duty to rescue a family member who was in trouble. Think of Boaz, who became the redeemer for Ruth. Job no longer has anyone on earth who will stand up for him. His friends don't believe he is innocent. His only remaining hope is God as his Redeemer.

More than anything, Job longs to see God. In Job 19:27 he says, literally, that his kidneys are consumed with longing within him. He wants so badly to see God! That is his deepest desire, and Job is completely sincere in it. He doesn't want to lose his relationship with God.

*A striking fact about the kidneys:* in neighboring Egypt, the brain was thrown away during mummification. Organs such as the liver and lungs were dried and stored in jars, but the kidneys, like the heart, were left inside the body. After all, they represented the inner person and had to stay with them!

Job's firm conviction that God will have the last word is what keeps him going. He clings to it now that everything around him has fallen away:

> “But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will stand upon the earth. After my skin is destroyed, then I will see God in my flesh.” (Job 19:25–26)

## 6. If Only I Could Find Him

**Read:** Job 23:1–7 · Job 23:10–12

Job is growing more and more frustrated. He doesn't understand. How can it be that God suddenly seems to have abandoned him? What exactly has he overlooked? Has he committed some sin, or is all this suffering simply here because God wills it? It makes him anxious and uncertain.

What he wants most is simply to go to God:

> “Oh that I knew where I might find him! That I might come even to his seat!” (Job 23:3)

To speak with Him heart to heart. To take God to court. All the facts laid out, Job in a courtroom with a well-prepared defense, standing before God. It's a very earthly way of thinking: when someone seems to have been treated unjustly, there should be a trial where both sides can explain themselves. That is how things were settled in Job's time, and it's how we handle problems in our society today, too.

All we hear, chapter after chapter, are Job and his friends responding to each other. The long conversations are almost exhausting. But Job so badly wants to know what God would say to him about all this. How recognizable that is for all of us. How often do we wish for a note from heaven with God's exact answer, or His opinion, about whatever we are struggling with?

Job says he would understand the answers God gave him. He trusts that God truly knows how much he loves Him. He is certain that if he is tested, he will come out like gold:

> “But he knows the way that I take. When he has tried me, I will come out like gold.” (Job 23:10)

I can just imagine him lining up all his arguments, playing out scenarios during sleepless nights, holding entire conversations in his head. His conviction is so firm that, gradually, his friends realize there is no point in confronting Job with his “unrighteousness.”

It is remarkable to remember that already in chapter 1, God called Job His servant, a blameless and upright man. If only Job knew…

## 7. Elihu and the Mediator

**Read:** Job 32:1–5 · Job 33:13 · Job 33:23–24

> So these three men ceased to answer Job, because he was righteous in his own eyes. Then the wrath of Elihu the son of Barachel, the Buzite, of the family of Ram, was kindled against Job. His wrath was kindled because he justified himself rather than God. Also his wrath was kindled against his three friends, because they had found no answer, and yet had condemned Job. Now Elihu had waited to speak to Job, because they were older than he. When Elihu saw that there was no answer in the mouth of these three men, his wrath was kindled. (Job 32:1–5)

Job's last friend seems to come out of nowhere. His name is Elihu, which means “He is my God.” He is the son of Barachel, a name that means “God blesses.” It's beautiful to realize that after Elihu's speech, God's blessing really does follow.

As the youngest of the friends, Elihu brings a completely different perspective. He starts off fiercely: his anger is directed at Job and at the other three friends alike. He has probably had to bite his tongue all this time while listening to the other speeches.

Elihu says:

> “Why do you strive against him, because he doesn't give account of any of his matters?” (Job 33:13)

He rightly points out where Job falls short. Job wants to take God to court as if He were his equal, but that is simply impossible. God's ways are so much higher than ours.

Then, in chapter 33, Elihu says something remarkable:

> “If there is beside him an angel, an interpreter, one among a thousand, to show to man what is right for him, then God is gracious to him, and says, ‘Deliver him from going down to the pit, I have found a ransom.’” (Job 33:23–24)

The word for *angel* here is the Hebrew *mal'ak*, which means messenger or envoy. The word for *interpreter* is *melits*, which means something like translator or ambassador. And the word for *ransom* is *kopher*: the price paid to set someone free.

Who does this remind you of? A unique envoy, one among a thousand, who mediates for us and pays the ransom so that we can be set free? I believe this points ahead to Christ, a foreshadowing like the many we find throughout the Old Testament. How remarkable that Elihu speaks of this long before Jesus Christ was born.

Job doesn't respond to Elihu. Instead, God Himself speaks, out of a storm.

## 8. God Speaks from the Storm

**Read:** Matthew 8:23–27 · Psalm 33:6–7 · Job 38 · Job 39:1–40:5

> When he got into a boat, his disciples followed him. Behold, a violent storm came up on the sea, so much that the boat was covered with the waves, but he was asleep. The disciples came to him, and woke him up, saying, “Save us, Lord! We are dying!” He said to them, “Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?” Then he got up, rebuked the wind and the sea, and there was a great calm. The men marveled, saying, “What kind of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” (Matthew 8:23–27)

> Then Yahweh answered Job out of the whirlwind, “Who is this who darkens counsel by words without knowledge? Brace yourself like a man, for I will question you, then you answer me! Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if you have understanding. Who determined its measures, if you know?” (Job 38:1–5)

God answers out of a storm. How overwhelming that must have been. I imagine Job standing rooted to the ground, full of awe. After all those speeches and exhausting conversations with his friends, after the deafening silence of the God he has been desperately searching for all this time, suddenly He is there. And God speaks.

God confronts Job directly about his “words without knowledge,” about the fact that Job has called Him to account while Job himself cannot answer any of the questions God now asks him. It almost sounds sarcastic: *Were you there, Job, when I laid the foundations of the earth?*

God shows that He rules over all of creation, including its unfathomable and incomprehensible parts. He is in complete control of the chaos Job is experiencing. An interesting detail: in the ancient Semitic world, control over the sea was seen as the ultimate symbol of divine power and authority. Think of Jesus in Matthew, calming the waves. Only God can do that! Through His words, God shows Job that He is a God of order. We see the same thing in the creation account in Genesis.

In Job 38:16, God asks Job:

> “Have you entered into the springs of the sea? Or have you walked in the recesses of the deep?”

These springs of the deep are already mentioned in Genesis 7:11, when the flood came. In 1977, scientists discovered hydrothermal vents: cracks in the ocean floor that pour out scalding hot water. A discovery for us, but long known to God…

God tells Job about light and darkness, and even about the stars, the laws of the heavens and how they rule. He points to all kinds of animals and their peculiar behaviors. Take the ostrich, for example, which doesn't protect her own eggs. Science has since discovered that her brain is exceptionally small. God reveals specific details of His creation that were still unknown to a man like Job and his contemporaries. God knows these animals through and through, because He created them so ingeniously.

Job has nothing to say in response to all this revealed wisdom. He humbles himself, because he realizes he is in no position to accuse God or to teach Him anything:

> “Behold, I am of small account. What will I answer you? I lay my hand on my mouth.” (Job 40:4)

## 9. Behemoth and Leviathan

**Read:** Job 40:15–24 · Job 41:1–9

*Note: some Bible translations, including many Dutch ones, number these verses differently. In those translations this passage is Job 40:10–28.*

God isn't finished with His answer to Job yet. He tells him about two impressive creatures He has made. Do you remember what Job said in chapter 3, verse 8, about Leviathan, and how it might as well be woken up to unleash chaos? God comes back to that, and how!

First He describes Behemoth, “the chief of the ways of God” (40:19). An enormously strong animal, yet a plant-eater. Some translations call it a hippopotamus, but the description doesn't quite seem to fit. If you look up pictures, you'll find all kinds of wondrous creatures, but we don't know exactly what kind of animal this was.

Then God goes on to describe Leviathan, whose name means “the twisting one.” What an impressive creature! It strongly resembles a dragon. Its skin is made of tightly fitting shields, and it breathes fire. Job 41:31 says:

> “He makes the deep to boil like a pot. He makes the sea like a pot of ointment.”

It reminds me of the sea monsters in pirate movies, causing chaos and panic wherever they go.

What is God actually trying to say here? Why is this His answer to Job's suffering? To us it feels distant: a description of an almost mythical creature we've never seen. How could this possibly help Job with his questions?

But if we put ourselves in the shoes of someone from Job's time, we see that this theme comes up again and again. It appears in Sumerian and Mesopotamian traditions: the battle between chaos and order. God is a God of order; He already shows that in creation. And when He speaks to Job, He speaks in terms of order. He has shown the dawn its place, He sets boundaries for the sea, He knows the laws of the heavens and the habits and character of every animal.

In effect, God is saying: *I am in complete control of every element of creation. I made all of it, I know it inside and out, and I rule over it.*

## 10. God Answers with His Presence

**Read:** Job 41:10–12 · Isaiah 27:1

> “None is so fierce that he dare stir him up. Who then is he who can stand before me? Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Everything under the heavens is mine. I will not keep silence concerning his limbs, nor his mighty strength, nor his goodly frame.” (Job 41:10–12)

God closes, as it were, with the grand finale: Leviathan, a creature beyond compare, “made without fear,” “king over all the sons of pride” (41:33–34). No human can control him. In fact, people are terrified of him and have no way to subdue him. And God created him. He is the only one who can handle Leviathan, because He made him, with every feature and trait he has.

In Isaiah 27:1 we read the prophecy of what will happen to him:

> “In that day, Yahweh with his hard and great and strong sword will punish leviathan, the fleeing serpent, and leviathan the twisted serpent; and he will kill the dragon that is in the sea.”

So God still has a plan for this monster, a plan that was settled long ago. But Job doesn't know that. For now, it is enough for Job to know that God is in control, precisely over all those frightening parts of creation. God shows that He holds everything in His hand, and He even seems proud of His creatures and everything that comes with them. Their existence doesn't threaten God; it doesn't throw anything into disarray.

This is exactly what Job was looking for. In his frustration he had wondered whether God was truly just, whether God really saw all the injustice happening on earth. And this is God's answer: *Yes, I am here. I am present. I see it. I hold everything in My hand. You are safe in Me.*

Perhaps, toward the end of the book, we expect a moment where all the puzzle pieces fall into place and Job's questions are finally answered. That doesn't happen. God answers with His presence.

## 11. Now My Eye Sees You

**Read:** Job 42:1–6

Job's response is moving. Do you remember what he said in chapter 19?

> “Whom I, even I, will see on my side. My eyes will see, and not as a stranger. My heart is consumed within me.” (Job 19:27)

Job's deep longing, felt so intensely that it nearly consumed him, comes true. In the end, he sees God in all His power and majesty. He says:

> “I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.” (Job 42:5)

It is remarkable to read how Job's questions and doubts seem to fall away completely at the sight of God in all His might. He doesn't ask, *But what about all the disaster that came over me?* And God says nothing about Satan's test either. Job is filled with God's glory, and his deepest wish has been fulfilled. He can entrust himself to this great God and has found rest in not knowing. God's presence is enough.

Today, too, there are people around us who go through terrible things. Sometimes you hear someone say, “When I get to heaven, I'll have a lot of questions for God!” From a human point of view, that makes perfect sense. We often don't understand why misery comes our way, why someone becomes terminally ill, why there is poverty and suffering.

God says that Job spoke rightly about Him, so it's okay to bring our complaints to God. We may pour out our hearts, our doubts, and our questions before Him. David does the same in the Psalms; in doing so, he is actually seeking connection with God. But Job's story also helps me imagine that those questions might simply fall away when we see our Maker. That all we will want then is to be with Him, because He is perfect.

## 12. Restored

**Read:** Job 42:7–17

All Job can do is repent. He accused God, and now it turns out he spoke without knowledge. His repentance is enough for God, who has seen Job's heart. But God is angry with Job's friends, because they did not speak rightly about Him, as His servant Job did. God only addresses the three friends, not Elihu, the young friend. That's something to think about: Elihu was the only one who pointed ahead to the Redeemer, the ambassador.

We might read over it quickly, but what God asks of Job here is no small thing. He must pray for his friends; otherwise God will not accept their burnt offerings. So Job has to pray so that his friends will not be punished. The very same friends who wounded him so deeply with their harsh words when he was already completely broken. With friends like these, who needs enemies? Many people would develop a deep aversion and never want to see them again, or would even wish that they really would be punished. But Job does it anyway: he prays for them. I think we see Job's righteousness here once again. He is a gentle man, and he forgives his friends.

The Lord turns Job's fortunes around. He gives him twice as much as he had before. His brothers and sisters come to visit him and bring gifts. Over time he has children again, seven sons and three daughters. They will never replace the children he lost, but they surely bring comfort. He is fully restored in honor. After this, he lives another 140 years and even sees his great-grandchildren. That is beautiful. Nothing like this is impossible for God. His restoration of Job shows His faithfulness and grace. But I believe Job's heart was already full: he had experienced God's justice. And he knows that whatever happens, God holds everything in His hand.

That message from Job is for us, too. God knows every detail of our lives, and we can entrust them to Him, even when we don't understand why. And because Jesus died for us, something that hadn't yet happened in Job's time, we know that when we put our trust in Him, we will ultimately have eternal life with Him. He is still our Redeemer!
