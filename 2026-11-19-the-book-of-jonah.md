*This study is made up of eleven short devotionals. Each one begins with the Bible passages to read first. Scripture quotations are from the World English Bible (WEB).*

## 1. Running Away

**Read:** Jonah 1:1–3 · 2 Kings 14:25 · Nahum 3:1–4 · Luke 11:29–32

Jonah was not a fictional character. He really existed. He is mentioned in 2 Kings 14:25 as a historical prophet. And his story, however strange and incomprehensible it may seem, really happened. Jesus Himself speaks about him in Luke:

> “This is an evil generation. It seeks after a sign. No sign will be given to it but the sign of Jonah, the prophet.” (Luke 11:29)

Jonah was a prophet from Gath Hepher, a small town in the territory of the tribe of Zebulun, in Galilee. He appears once before in the Bible, in 2 Kings 14:25, where he prophesies during the reign of King Jeroboam II. So Jonah had spoken a favorable prophecy that came true and that benefited Israel.

And now God called him to go to Israel's archenemy, the Assyrians. According to Genesis 10, Nineveh was a city founded by Nimrod, the mighty hunter of Genesis and a great-grandson of Noah. He had also founded Babel. The Assyrian empire, of which Nineveh was part, was enormous and unprecedentedly cruel. There are ancient accounts of enemies being tortured and killed in horrific ways. On excavated palace reliefs and in royal inscriptions, the Assyrians themselves describe how brutally they treated their enemies. In Nahum 3:1–4, Nineveh is called a “bloody city,” full of lies, robbery, and witchcraft.

I can imagine that Jonah must have been very afraid that the same thing would happen to him, especially with such an unwelcome message to deliver. But the text says nothing about that. Jonah has an important reason that we don't read about yet; it only comes out at the end of the book.

Jonah decides to flee. He wants to get as far away from Nineveh as possible. Nineveh lies to the east of Israel, so instead he chooses a place far to the west (probably in present-day Spain). He boards a ship bound for Tarshish, a long sea route that was certainly not without danger.

*An interesting detail:* in Jonah 1 and 2, the word “went down” (Hebrew *yarad*) appears again and again. Jonah goes down to Joppa, down into the ship, and down into the hold (1:3, 5), and finally “down to the bottoms of the mountains” (2:6). Then God brings him up. The story is literally a downward movement that God reverses.

**Reflect:** It's striking: Jonah knows God's voice, and yet he runs as far away from it as he can. Is there something you know God is asking of you, but that you would rather run away from?

## 2. Asleep in the Storm

**Read:** Jonah 1:4–10 · Psalm 139:7–10

The sailors Jonah traveled with were surely no amateurs. Sea travel in those days was risky, but these were professionals who had probably seen a thing or two. Yet now a storm arose so violent that even these seasoned men were terrified. Each of them cried out to his own god. In that time, people worshiped many different gods, often connected to the elements: there were gods of the wind and gods of the water.

It's a strange detail that Jonah, of all people, falls into a deep sleep right then. In all that raging water, on a ship that is about to break apart? The captain is stunned. How can anyone sleep like this? He tells Jonah to call on his God too:

> “What do you mean, sleeper? Arise, call on your God! Maybe your God will notice us, so that we won't perish.” (Jonah 1:6)

Maybe then they would stand a better chance of surviving!

The sailors want to know who is to blame for this disaster. That's an unusual question, one we would rarely ask in our modern society. We might ask “why?” or “how can this happen?”, but we are far removed from the ancient Near Eastern culture in which extreme circumstances or accidents were immediately attributed to a god. So they cast lots to find out who was guilty. Casting lots was common in those days, and people believed that the divine determined the outcome. As Proverbs 16:33 says:

> “The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from Yahweh.”

The lot falls on Jonah. The men immediately want to know where he comes from and what he does. Jonah answers:

> “I am a Hebrew, and I fear Yahweh, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land.” (Jonah 1:9)

He calls God by His personal name, *Yahweh*. And at the same time there is a contradiction: he fears Yahweh, the Creator of sea and land, and yet he chooses to flee across the sea, the very work of his God's hands. Against his better judgment! And as a prophet, Jonah must have known the Torah well. It brings to mind Psalm 139:

> “If I take the wings of the dawn, and settle in the uttermost parts of the sea, even there your hand will lead me, and your right hand will hold me.” (Psalm 139:9–10)

The name Yahweh fills the sailors with awe and fear. How could Jonah have done this?

**Reflect:** While everyone around him felt the danger, Jonah slept. Where might you have become less sensitive to God's voice, His calling, or the needs of others?

## 3. Digging Through Walls

**Read:** Jonah 1:11–13 · Amos 9:2–3

It's remarkable how these men, who don't even know Yahweh, cry out to Jonah:

> “What shall we do to you, that the sea may be calm to us?” (Jonah 1:11)

They sense that this storm is the judgment of a powerful God, so much greater than the gods they have called on in vain. They clearly realize this battle is between God and Jonah. The extreme weather can be blamed on only one person: the disobedient Jonah.

The men first try something else:

> Nevertheless the men rowed hard to get them back to the land; but they could not, for the sea grew more and more stormy against them. (Jonah 1:13)

The Hebrew verb used here for “rowed hard” is *ḥātar*. It appears only eight times in the entire Hebrew Bible. In six of those eight cases it has nothing to do with water; it literally means “to dig through walls.” Jonah 1:13 is the only place in the Bible where the word is used for rowing on the water. The only other place where *ḥātar* is not used for digging through the walls of houses is Amos 9:2. There it describes people trying to escape God's judgment by “digging into Sheol,” the underworld. A striking wordplay. Digging suggests hard physical labor, but also something nearly impossible.

Amos 9 continues this prophecy by saying that even if those who flee hide at the bottom of the sea, God will command the serpent (*nāḥāš*) to bite them:

> “Though they hide themselves on the top of Carmel, I will search and take them out from there; and though they be hidden from my sight in the bottom of the sea, there I will command the serpent, and it will bite them.” (Amos 9:3)

The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, translates the word for serpent here as *drakōn*, dragon, which fits the image of a sea monster like Leviathan.

By the way, Amos prophesied under the same king, Jeroboam II, as Jonah did (Amos 1:1). While Jonah foretold the expansion of Israel's borders, Amos announced judgment on Israel!

What exactly is Leviathan? We'll come back to that later, so be patient a little longer. Either way, the same imagery shows up in both books. In Jonah and in Amos, we see a desperate attempt to flee from God's presence and judgment. And that attempt fails, because God is almighty. It is a thread that runs through the whole Bible.

**Reflect:** If there truly is no place beyond God's reach, what does your urge to run say about how you see God?

## 4. Overboard

**Read:** Jonah 1:14–16

The sailors are desperate. They have tried to row back to shore, but they can't. It's as if they have to dig through a wall (*ḥātar*). I imagine God's intervention in the elements was so powerful that there was simply no way through, as if some kind of cosmic shield had been raised.

The men don't want to die because of Jonah's choice to flee from his God. Their only option is to throw Jonah overboard. But that would mean Jonah's immediate death. The sea is raging! These “pagans” show more compassion for one Hebrew than Jonah will soon show for an entire city.

Something remarkable happens here. The sailors, who were first terrified of the sea, end up far more afraid of Jonah's God. They understand where this terrible storm is coming from, and the last thing they want is to bring the wrath of this God upon themselves:

> “We beg you, Yahweh, we beg you, don't let us die for this man's life, and don't lay on us innocent blood; for you, Yahweh, have done as it pleased you.” (Jonah 1:14)

All wisdom begins with the fear of God (Proverbs 1:7).

In the end, Jonah is thrown into the sea. It says something about Jonah and his faith that he is willing to sacrifice himself for the lives of these men. He doesn't keep running from God. He knows it's pointless, and he acknowledges and accepts God's intervention. Of course, Jonah didn't know how it would end. He let himself be thrown into the churning sea, toward his death. It reminds us of Jesus, who also went into judgment willingly. But with one great difference: Jonah goes into the depths because of his own disobedience, Jesus because of the sins of others.

Some see courage and self-sacrifice in Jonah here; others see a prophet who would rather die than obey. The text leaves it open.

And isn't it remarkable that despite Jonah's flight and rebellion, an entire ship's crew now comes to faith in the true God? They end up worshiping the Lord. God can turn any situation around for good.

Whoever runs from God discovers that there is no place beyond His reach. That is a warning, but also a comfort: even in the depths, He is there.

> “If I ascend up into heaven, you are there. If I make my bed in Sheol, behold, you are there!” (Psalm 139:8)

**Reflect:** There is no place where God doesn't see you and no depth where He can't reach you. Is that mainly a comfort to you, or something you would rather run away from?

## 5. The Great Fish

**Read:** Jonah 1:17 · Matthew 12:40 · Luke 11:30

> Yahweh prepared a huge fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of the fish three days and three nights. (Jonah 1:17)

This is a special verse, one we shouldn't read over too quickly. If we dive into the original text, we see that the Hebrew says *dag gadol*, which is translated as “great fish.” But the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible) uses the word *kētos*, which means “sea monster” or “great sea creature.” Matthew uses the same Greek word as the Septuagint:

> “For as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the huge fish (*kētos*), so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.” (Matthew 12:40)

Why does this matter? For Greek readers, *kētos* carried a much richer meaning than simply a big fish. It was a sea monster that symbolized chaos: the forces that oppose the order God brings. And we find exactly that imagery elsewhere in the Bible. Think of Leviathan in Job 41, the awe-inspiring sea creature no human can tame. Think of Psalm 74:14, where God crushes the heads of Leviathan, or Isaiah 27:1, where Leviathan appears as an image of the evil that God will finally judge.

Over time, we have lost the meaning of this image. But in earlier centuries, this *kētos* appears again and again in sculpture and painting, as early as the third century AD. The chaos monster is often depicted with a twisting serpent's body and a dog-like head full of teeth. A terrifying beast, and surely one of a kind even in those days.

It is striking that the fish is masculine in Jonah 1:17 (*dag*) and feminine in Jonah 2:1 (*dagah*). In Psalm 139:15, David describes how he was formed by God “in the depths of the earth” (*taḥtiyyot ereṣ*), an image for the hidden place of the mother's womb. For Jonah, the inside of the fish becomes a starting point from which he eventually emerges as a “new” person. More about *ereṣ* in part 6.

Back to Jesus. He refers to the story of Jonah. In Luke, Jesus even says that Jonah himself was the sign:

> “For even as Jonah became a sign to the Ninevites, so the Son of Man will also be to this generation.” (Luke 11:30)

Jonah *is* the message. That may be one reason why, in art and reliefs, Jonah is sometimes depicted without a scroll, unlike the other prophets.

Jesus takes the comparison deeper. Jonah was deep in the belly of the *kētos*, the chaos monster. That was his judgment. Jesus went to meet judgment for our sins, and after His death on the cross He remained in the grave for three days. Through His resurrection, every person can be saved. Jesus is so much more than Jonah.

## 6. Out of the Depths

**Read:** Micah 7:19 · Psalm 42:7 · Jonah 2:1–10

*Note: some Bible translations, including many Dutch ones, number the verses of Jonah 2 and Psalm 42 one higher than English Bibles do.*

Jonah goes through a terrible struggle with death in the belly of this sea monster. In chapter 2 we read how he experiences it:

> “I called because of my affliction to Yahweh. He answered me. Out of the belly of Sheol I cried. You heard my voice. For you threw me into the depths, in the heart of the seas. The flood was all around me. All your waves and your billows passed over me.” (Jonah 2:2–3)

*Sheol* is the Hebrew word for the realm of the dead. The word for “depths” is *metsulah*. It's the same word used in Job 41:31, where God says of Leviathan: “He makes the deep to boil like a pot.” This particular word appears only twelve times in the Bible, always for the deep, deadly sea. For example, in Exodus 15:5, about Pharaoh's army drowning in the sea. But in Micah 7:19 it is used for the place where God throws our sins, in a passage full of grace:

> “He will again have compassion on us. He will tread our iniquities under foot; and you will cast all their sins into the depths of the sea.” (Micah 7:19)

What does this mean? Once again it's the same imagery, and it strengthens the picture of the *kētos* that swallows Jonah. Jonah is thrown into the depths, the very depths where this sea monster lives. He also says:

> “I went down to the bottoms of the mountains. The earth barred me in forever: yet you have brought my life up from the pit, Yahweh my God.” (Jonah 2:6)

The Hebrew word for “earth” here is *ereṣ*. In the Bible, *ereṣ* is sometimes used for the underworld or the realm of the dead, the place from which no one returns. For example, in 1 Samuel 28:13, where the spirit of Samuel is called up “out of the earth.” The bars that close forever fit seamlessly into that picture of the realm of the dead: Jonah cannot escape.

Jonah prays almost entirely in the language of the Psalms. Jonah 2:3b is word for word Psalm 42:7: “All your waves and your billows have swept over me.” There are also echoes of Psalms 18, 31, and 120. The prophet who ran from God still carries the Psalms in his heart, there in the depths of the sea. His prayer contains no confession of guilt, but it does include promises to bring offerings to God.

But just as Jonah's soul is fainting, God's saving help arrives. God hears Jonah's prayer.

> Then Yahweh spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land. (Jonah 2:10)

How amazing that this chaos monster obeys immediately, unlike Jonah himself, and spits him out.

**Reflect:** In his deepest need, Jonah drew on the Psalms. Which words of God carry you when your own words fall short?

## 7. Five Words

**Read:** Jonah 3:1–5

> Yahweh's word came to Jonah the second time, saying, “Arise, go to Nineveh, that great city, and preach to it the message that I give you.” So Jonah arose, and went to Nineveh, according to Yahweh's word. Now Nineveh was an exceedingly great city, three days' journey across. Jonah began to enter into the city a day's journey, and he cried out, and said, “In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!” The people of Nineveh believed God. (Jonah 3:1–5)

Wow, this happens fast. Wasn't Nineveh an extremely godless and dangerous city? The Assyrians were cruel and Israel's archenemy, notorious for the horrific way they treated their opponents. How is it possible that Jonah says only five words (*od arba'im yom veNinveh nehpachet*) and suddenly everyone listens to him?

It's important to look closely at this text. *Nehpachet* (“overthrown”) is the verb used for the destruction of Sodom (Genesis 19:25), but it can also mean “turned around, changed.” Nineveh is indeed turned around, just not in the way Jonah expected.

Nineveh was one of the great cities of Assyria and later the capital of the Neo-Assyrian empire, in what is now Iraq. Its people believed in the *Apkallu*: seven ancient, semi-divine beings who passed on hidden knowledge to humanity. These figures were depicted on reliefs in Assyrian palace art, and some of them can still be seen in museums today. One of these Apkallu was called Oannes, a being who wore a fish skin draped over him like a cloak. Berossus, a Babylonian priest from the third century BC, describes this figure: he came up out of the sea to teach people civilization and the arts.

If we place the story of Jonah in this context, it may help explain the awe the people showed and why they accepted his message right away. It is unlikely that Jonah was spat out on the coast unseen and anonymously, since harbor towns were usually busy and full of trade. And think of the way it happened: a man spat out by such a terrifying sea monster. How much attention would that draw? Jonah probably didn't look very fresh after three days inside the fish, either.

Another possible explanation is that, in the period in which many scholars place Jonah, Assyria was struck by a severe plague (765 BC), a total solar eclipse (763 BC), and internal unrest. In ancient Near Eastern thinking, events like these were signs of divine anger, and the Assyrians took omens extremely seriously. So Jonah may have arrived at exactly the right moment: a foreign prophet appears and announces judgment in forty days, just as the empire has gone through years of disaster.

**Reflect:** Five words, and an entire city turns around. If God could bring a pagan world city to repentance with five words, why do we so often doubt the power of His Word?

## 8. A City Turns Around

**Read:** Jonah 3:5–10

The shaken people of Nineveh believed Jonah's message and took action right away, old and young alike. The whole city was in uproar, and normal life came to a complete standstill. Then the news reached the king of this mighty empire. What a statement: he takes off his royal robe and puts on sackcloth, a public expression of deep remorse. He even orders that all people and animals must fast. No food and no water. That must have had quite an impact, not least on all those animals that couldn't understand why. Animals in mourning clothes also appear outside the Bible, in Persian mourning customs (described by Herodotus) and in Judith 4:10. It shows how deep Nineveh's repentance went.

Everyone must turn “from the violence that is in his hands” (3:8). That description shows once again how far this people had strayed. It must have been a bizarre sight: such a great city, with more than 120,000 inhabitants, completely shut down, with people neither eating nor drinking and crying out loudly to God.

This cruel empire, whose wickedness had “come up before” God (1:2), makes a radical turnaround, in the hope that God will turn from His anger. And God sees it:

> God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. God relented of the disaster which he said he would do to them, and he didn't do it. (Jonah 3:10)

Through their actions, they showed that they recognized how wicked they had been. God does not punish the city. Nineveh escapes its judgment.

**Reflect:** God's judgment was not the last word; His mercy was. Where in your life is He still waiting for you to turn around?

## 9. Angry at Grace

**Read:** Exodus 34:6 · Deuteronomy 18:21–22 · Jonah 4:1–4

Jonah is furious.

> But it displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry. He prayed to Yahweh, and said, “Please, Yahweh, wasn't this what I said when I was still in my own country? Therefore I hurried to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that you are a gracious God, and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm. Therefore now, Yahweh, take, I beg you, my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.” (Jonah 4:1–3)

How is it possible that this thoroughly corrupt city, with its wicked and violent people, isn't punished at all? Do they just get away with it? Jonah had feared this. In fact, he had already said it to God. It's one of the reasons he fled to Tarshish.

This is remarkable: Jonah is angry about God's mercy, His loving kindness, His grace. His words are almost a direct quotation of Exodus 34:6, where God appears to Moses. Normally this verse is quoted as praise, but Jonah uses it as a complaint against God. The same Jonah who had just praised God for that very same loving kindness when God saved him from the sea monster! He doesn't want mercy for murderers. He wants punishment.

We can imagine that Jonah now feels his horrific, days-long brush with death was all “for nothing.” Another factor may be that his prophecy against Nineveh didn't come true. In those days, that could get you accused of being a false prophet (see Deuteronomy 18:22). His reputation and his ego may well have been at stake. On top of that, the threat to Israel, his own country, would remain. In fact, around 732 BC Assyria conquered Galilee and Zebulun, Jonah's own region (2 Kings 15:29; Isaiah 9:1). Something Jonah himself could not have known at the time.

We also don't know whether Jonah truly believed the people's public repentance was real. Either way, none of this is explained in the text. We don't know, and we can only guess at the real reason for Jonah's bitterness.

None of it seems to matter to Jonah anymore. He would rather die than live, that's how deeply he is shaken.

*An interesting detail:* the word *ra'ah* (“evil”) keeps coming back. Nineveh's evil rises up before God (1:2). Nineveh turns from its evil way, and God turns from the evil (disaster) He would have done (3:8–10). And then the text literally says: “it was a great evil to Jonah” (4:1). The evil that Nineveh lets go of now has Jonah firmly in its grip.

And God asks:

> “Is it right for you to be angry?” (Jonah 4:4)

A rhetorical question that invites Jonah to take a look in the mirror.

**Reflect:** What question might God ask you about your anger toward people you think deserve punishment?

## 10. The Plant, the Worm, and the Wind

**Read:** Jonah 4:5–11

Jonah withdraws, probably deeply disappointed.

> Then Jonah went out of the city, and sat on the east side of the city, and there made himself a booth, and sat under it in the shade, until he might see what would become of the city. (Jonah 4:5)

He sits in a shady spot with a good view of the city. He wants to know whether something might still happen to it after all.

God makes a plant grow, called *qîqāyôn* in the original text, probably a castor oil plant. It's a poisonous plant that grows quickly (up to about thirteen feet tall) and has large leaves. Jonah is very happy with it, because the eastern sun must have been intense.

But the next day God appoints a worm that attacks the plant. The word “appointed” (or “prepared”) is interesting. First God appoints the sea creature, then the plant to grow, then the worm to destroy it. God rules over every element of creation and can use any of them as His instrument. Then God also appoints a scorching east wind. We see that same east wind as God's instrument in Exodus 14:21 and Hosea 13:15.

Jonah finds it unbearable, without shade in the heat and with that scorching wind on top of it. He longs to die. It's almost as if the same conversation is happening all over again. Jonah says it is better for him to die than to live. And God asks:

> “Is it right for you to be angry about the vine?” He said, “I am right to be angry, even to death.” (Jonah 4:9)

A pointed question, which Jonah answers bitterly. Then God shows him how absurd the situation is:

> “You have been concerned for the vine, for which you have not labored, neither made it grow; which came up in a night, and perished in a night. Shouldn't I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also much livestock?” (Jonah 4:10–11)

The plant came up in one night and died in one night. Jonah didn't have to do anything for it. Yet he feels so much about it, and nothing at all at the thought of more than 120,000 people, plus all the animals in the city, being destroyed. It's a morally twisted way of thinking, and God rightly points it out. God describes Jonah's feelings for the plant with the word *ḥūs* (“to have pity, to spare”) and uses that very same word for His own feelings toward Nineveh.

We don't read any answer from Jonah. The story ends very abruptly, with an open ending. We don't know what happened to Jonah after this. But if we read the Bible carefully, we can read about the future of Nineveh.

**Reflect:** The book doesn't end with Jonah's answer, but with God's question. Maybe because that question isn't only meant for Jonah. How would you answer it?

## 11. Nineveh's End

**Read:** Nahum 1:1–3 · Exodus 34:7 · Nahum 3:1–7 · Nahum 3:18–19

The story of Jonah takes place in the eighth century BC. Nahum is usually dated between 663 and 612 BC. He is an unknown prophet about whom we know very little, but his name means “comfort.” He speaks out his vision of Nineveh's downfall. And Nineveh's final destruction took place in 612 BC.

The people of the city in Jonah's time had turned from their evil ways. But the generations that followed went back to their old way of life, full of violence and bloodshed. Nahum 3 speaks of a city that is “all full of lies and robbery,” with a multitude of dead bodies. They hadn't learned much.

So was Jonah's whole journey for nothing? No. In his day, 120,000 people and their livestock were spared, and God's character was revealed. All those lives that were given a chance: that was of immeasurable value. God is gracious enough to postpone an announced judgment when people repent. But it is exactly as Nahum says:

> “Yahweh is slow to anger, and great in power, and will by no means leave the guilty unpunished.” (Nahum 1:3)

Do you remember how Jonah quoted Exodus 34:6 about God's character? Nahum quotes the very next verse. He highlights the other side of God. God has been very patient, but now His patience has run out. Nineveh has once again chosen violence, and God does not close His eyes to injustice. In Nahum, God now announces Nineveh's final destruction. The last verse of Nahum says it all:

> “There is no healing your wound, for your injury is fatal. All who hear the report of you clap their hands over you; for who hasn't felt your endless cruelty?” (Nahum 3:19)

Just imagine how many victims this regime left in the surrounding lands. A true reign of terror. Everyone is deeply relieved: the Lord has brought justice.

And with that, we have come to the end of this study. There is so much in this fascinating story, and there is still so much more to discover. The question that lingers after reading the book of Jonah, and perhaps after this study too, may be this:

**How do we respond when God shows grace to people we think don't really deserve it?**
